The Hemp Law Is Changing.
Here Are the Facts.

A plain-language, fully sourced guide to the 2018 Farm Bill, the 2025 law that rewrote it, and what the November 12, 2026 hemp definition change means for the non-intoxicating CBD & CBG that families rely on.

⏳— until enforcement begins (Nov 12, 2026)

The 60-Second Version

  • What: A 2025 federal law rewrote the definition of "hemp," changing it from a delta-9-only standard to a total-THC standard and capping finished products at 0.4 mg total THC per container.[3][4]
  • When: Signed November 12, 2025; enforcement begins November 12, 2026 — a one-year runway to adapt or seek a fix.[1][2]
  • The catch: Industry & policy analysts estimate it would make ~90–95% of hemp products federally unlawful — including most full-spectrum CBD, not just intoxicating "gas-station" items.[4][5]
  • The opening: The executive branch itself acknowledged the language would "inadvertently recriminalize full-spectrum non-intoxicating CBD" and asked Congress to fix it.[5] That's the reform window we're advocating in.

How We Got Here

  • 2018 — The Farm Bill
    The Agriculture Improvement Act defined hemp as cannabis with ≤0.3% delta-9 THC by dry weight, legalizing CBD and hemp farming. Because it named only delta-9, it unintentionally left the door open for delta-8, THCA, and other cannabinoids.[1]
  • 2018–2025 — The "loophole" market
    An estimated $28B market of hemp-derived intoxicants (delta-8 vapes, THCA flower, etc.) grew with little federal oversight, prompting pushback from lawmakers and state officials.[1]
  • November 12, 2025 — The law changes
    Section 781 of H.R. 5371 (Continuing Appropriations Act, 2026) was signed, rewriting the hemp definition. It passed inside a must-pass government-funding bill rather than standalone debate.[2][3]
  • November 12, 2026 — Enforcement begins
    The new total-THC definition and 0.4 mg cap take effect. As of now, absent a legislative fix, ~90–95% of current products become federally unlawful.[2][4]

An honest nuance: "Full-spectrum" CBD often contains a trace, non-intoxicating amount of THC that is essential to how the product works — and that trace is exactly what pushes many wellness products past the 0.4 mg cap. This is why advocates argue for smart regulation (age limits, testing, dose caps on intoxicants) rather than a blanket cap that erases non-intoxicating medicine.[5]

What the Science Actually Says

We only cite what's supportable. Here's the honest state of the evidence for the non-intoxicating cannabinoids at the heart of this fight:

CBD for seizures — strongest evidence

Purified CBD (Epidiolex) is FDA-approved for seizures associated with Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, Dravet syndrome, and tuberous sclerosis complex in patients 1 year and older — the first and only FDA-approved prescription CBD.[7][8] This is real, peer-reviewed, regulator-backed science.

FDA approved Epidiolex June 25, 2018 (Dravet & LGS), later adding TSC.[8]

CBD for anxiety — promising, still maturing

A widely cited review in Neurotherapeutics found existing evidence supports CBD's potential for generalized anxiety, panic, social anxiety, OCD, and PTSD — strongest for acute dosing, with fewer long-term studies.[9] We describe this as "promising," not "proven."

CBG for anxiety & stress — early clinical signal

The first human clinical trial of CBG (Washington State University, Scientific Reports, 2024) found 20 mg of hemp-derived CBG significantly reduced anxiety and stress vs. placebo, without intoxication.[10] One small, rigorous study — encouraging, and a reason not to ban it.

Where we stay careful: Evidence for cannabinoids in bipolar disorder and BPD is limited, and THC specifically can worsen mania or psychosis in some people. We share lived experiences for those conditions as personal stories, never as treatment claims — and we always encourage working with a licensed provider and never stopping prescribed medication on your own.
